> ## Q: What are G, n and h in the specification of an EC. eg See https://std.neuromancer.sk/nist/P-256

For an elliptic curve (EC) used in cryptography, the parameters **G**, **n**, and **h** define the subgroup in which cryptographic operations are performed. Using **NIST P-256** as an example:

* **G** — **Base point** (also called the **generator point**)

  * This is a specific point on the elliptic curve that everyone agrees to use.
  * Public keys are generated by multiplying this point by a private key:

    $$Q = dG$$

    where:
    * $d$ is the private key (an integer),
    * $Q$ is the public key (a point on the curve).

  On the P-256 specification page, **G** is given by its coordinates:

$$
G = (G_x, G_y)
$$
  
  where `Gx` and `Gy` are large hexadecimal numbers.

---

* **n** — **Order of the base point**

  * This is the number of distinct multiples of (G) before you return to the point at infinity.
  * Formally,

  $$nG = \mathcal{O}$$

    where $\mathcal{O}$ is the point at infinity (the identity element).

  For P-256,

$$
n = \texttt{FFFFFFFF00000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFBCE6FAADA7179E84F3B9CAC2FC632551}
$$

  which is a 256-bit prime.

  This means there are exactly $n$ points in the subgroup generated by $G$.

  The private key is chosen as

  $$
  1 \le d < n.
  $$

---

* **h** — **Cofactor**

  * The cofactor relates the size of the whole curve to the subgroup generated by $G$:

  $h = \frac{\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})}{n}$

    where $\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})$ is the total number of points on the curve (including the point at infinity).

  For P-256,

  $$h = 1$$

  This means:

  * every point on the curve is already in the subgroup generated by $G$,
  * there are no extra "small" subgroups to worry about,
  * implementations are simpler and safer.

---

### Why these parameters matter

Suppose Alice chooses a private key

$$
d = 123456789.
$$

Her public key is computed as

$$
Q = dG.
$$

Here:

* **G** tells everyone which generator to use.
* **n** tells Alice the valid range for $d$ and determines the subgroup size (which underpins the security level).
* **h** tells implementations whether they need to perform extra subgroup checks. Since P-256 has $h=1$, subgroup membership is straightforward.

---

### Summary

| Parameter | Meaning                                                            | P-256                   |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------| ----------------------- |
| **G**     | Generator (base) point used to derive public keys                  | Fixed point $(G_x,G_y)$ |
| **n**     | Order of $G$: smallest positive integer such that $nG=\mathcal{O}$ | Large 256-bit prime     |
| **h**     | Cofactor $=\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})/n$                                   | 1                       |

These three parameters, together with the field prime $p$ and the curve coefficients $a$ and $b$, completely specify the elliptic curve domain parameters used by protocols such as ECDSA and ECDH.


> ## Q: Will n always be prime for an EC?

No. **In general, the order $n$ of a point on an elliptic curve does not have to be prime.** However, in **cryptographic elliptic curves**, the chosen base point $G$ almost always has **prime order** (or an order that is a large prime times a very small cofactor).

It's helpful to distinguish three related quantities:

1. **The order of the curve**

$$
\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p),
$$
   
   which is the total number of points on the curve.

2. **The order of a point $P$**

$$
ord(P),
$$
   
   which is the smallest positive integer $m$ such that

$$
mP = \mathcal{O}.
$$

3. **The order $n$ in the domain parameters**
   This is specifically the order of the chosen generator $G$:

$$
n = ord(G).
$$

### A non-prime example

Suppose a curve has $\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = 60$

Then it might contain points of orders

* 2
* 3
* 4
* 5
* 6
* 10
* 12
* 15
* 20
* 30
* 60

depending on the group structure. If you chose a point of order 20 as your generator, then

$$
n = 20,
$$

which is **not prime**.

Mathematically, that's perfectly valid.

### Why cryptography prefers prime $n$

Most ECC schemes (ECDSA, ECDH, EdDSA, etc.) want the subgroup generated by $G$ to be **cyclic of large prime order** because:

* Every non-zero scalar has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$, which many algorithms rely on.
* There are no non-trivial subgroups inside a prime-order group, avoiding many small-subgroup attacks.
* The discrete logarithm problem has the expected hardness.

This is why standards typically choose

$$
\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = h \cdot n,
$$

where:

* $n$ is a **large prime**, and
* $h$ (the cofactor) is very small, usually 1, 2, 4, or 8.

For example:

| Curve      | $n$ prime? | Cofactor $h$ |
| ---------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| NIST P-256 | Yes        | 1            |
| secp256k1  | Yes        | 1            |
| Curve25519 | Yes        | 8            |
| Ed25519    | Yes        | 8            |

For Ed25519, the **entire curve group** is not prime-order—it has order $8n$—but the standard base point generates a subgroup of large prime order $n$.

So the answer is:

* **Mathematically:** No, $n$ can be composite.
* **In cryptographic standards:** $n$ is almost always chosen to be a large prime (or, equivalently, the generator is chosen to have large prime order), because that provides the algebraic properties and security required by ECC protocols.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Hasse-Weil bound aka Hasse's Theorem:
> 
> $p + 1 − 2p^{1/2} \leq \\#E\left(F_p \\!\\!​\\!\right) \leq p + 1 + 2p^{1/2}$
>
> Essentially, $\\#E(F_p) \approx p$ ie the number of elements in the Group $E(F_p)$ (points on the EC $E$ over the Field $F_p$) is roughly $p$, a little less or a little more.
>
> Also, $\\#E(F_p)$ is just another notation for $|E(F_p)|$
>
> Hence, Cardinality of $E(F_p)$ $\equiv$ $|E(F_p)| \equiv \\#E(F_p) \approx p = |F_p|$ $\equiv$ Cardinality of $F_p$
>
> $F_p$ is a Field (and hence a Group, because every Field is a Group). The operations are modular $\pmod{p}$ addition and modular $\pmod{p}$ multiplication.
>
> $E(F_p)$ is a Group (but not a Field). It contains the points on the EC $E$ over the Field $F_p$. The operation is "addition" of two points/elements.
>
> Field $F_p \equiv \left\\{0,1,2,...,p-1\right\\}$
>
> Group $E(F_p) \equiv \left\\{ (x,y) \in F_p \times F_p : (x,y) \text{ lies on } E \right\\}$
>


> ## Q: When you say "standards choose n to be a very large prime", it still can't be greater than p, correct? Because everything is (mod p).

Great question. The answer is **no: $n$ is not restricted to be less than $p$**. This is a very common point of confusion because the field arithmetic is done "mod $p$", but $n$ is a **group order**, not a field element.

Let's separate the two.

### 1. $p$ defines the field

The prime $p$ defines the field:

$$
\mathbb F_p = {0,1,\ldots,p-1}.
$$

All **coordinates** of elliptic curve points are elements of this field:

$$
x,y \in \mathbb F_p.
$$

So yes, coordinates are always reduced modulo $p$.

---

### 2. $n$ is the order of a group element

The value $n$ is defined by:

$$
nG = \mathcal O
$$

where $G$ is the generator point and $\mathcal O$ is the point at infinity.

This is repeated **point addition**:

$$
G,\quad 2G,\quad 3G,\quad \ldots,\quad nG=\mathcal O.
$$

The numbers $1,2,3,\ldots,n$ here are **integers counting how many times you add $G$**. They are not field elements.

So there is no rule saying:

$$
n < p.
$$

---

### Example

Take a small hypothetical curve:

$$
p=17.
$$

The field has:

$$
|\mathbb F_{17}|=17.
$$

The curve might have:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_{17})=19.
$$

A generator $G$ could have order:

$$
n=19.
$$

Here:

$$
n > p.
$$

Nothing is wrong. The curve group has 19 elements even though the field has only 17 elements.

The coordinates are modulo 17, but the number of points in the group can be larger.

---

### Why this happens

The field gives possible coordinates:

$$
(x,y)\in \mathbb F_p^2.
$$

There are $p^2$ possible pairs.

The curve selects some of those pairs, and Hasse's (bound) Theorem tells us:

$$
p+1 - ∂ ≤ \\#E(\mathbb F_p) ≤ p+1 + ∂
$$

where $∂ = 2√p$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> So the group order is naturally around $p$, but it can be slightly larger or smaller.
>

---

### Real example: NIST P-256

For P-256:

$$
p =
\texttt{FFFFFFFF00000001000000000000000000000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFF}
$$

and

$$
n =
\texttt{FFFFFFFF00000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFBCE6FAADA7179E84F3B9CAC2FC632551}
$$

Both are 256-bit numbers, but they are different. In fact, for P-256:

$$
n < p.
$$

For other curves, $n$ can be slightly larger than $p$. The only strict relationship is:

$$
n \mid \\#E(\mathbb F_p),
$$

because $n$ is the size of a subgroup of the elliptic curve group.

So the clean mental model is:

* $p$: size of the **field** (where coordinates live).
* $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$: size of the **elliptic curve group** (number of points).
* $n$: size of the **subgroup generated by $G$**.
* The phrase "mod $p$" applies to field arithmetic, not to the integer $n$.

> ## Q: So what are the ideal properties that n must have? Is the goal to make h as close to 1 as we can, or n as large as we can? Or something else?

Excellent question. The answer is: **both matter, but the primary goal is to make $n$ a very large prime and make the cofactor $h$ very small (ideally 1).** It is not simply "maximize $n$" or "make $h$ close to 1"; it is about achieving a secure group structure.

The ideal relationship is:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=h\cdot n
$$

where:

* $n$ = order of the subgroup generated by $G$
* $h$ = cofactor
* $G$ = generator of the subgroup

The desired properties are:

---

### 1. $n$ should be a large prime

This is the most important property.

Why?

Because ECC security relies on the **elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP)**:

Given:

$$
Q=dG
$$

it should be infeasible to find $d$.

The difficulty depends primarily on the size of the subgroup:

$$
|\langle G\rangle|=n.
$$

If $n$ is prime and very large:

* every non-zero private key $d$ has an inverse modulo $n$,
* there are no smaller subgroups inside $\langle G\rangle$,
* attacks are much harder.

For a 256-bit curve, you want roughly:

$$
n \approx 2^{256}
$$

with $n$ prime.

---

### 2. $h$ should be small (ideally $1$)

The cofactor is:

$$
h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}.
$$

A large $h$ means the curve has many points outside the subgroup generated by $G$.

Those extra subgroups can create problems, especially **small subgroup attacks**.

Ideal case:

$$
h=1
$$

Then:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=n
$$

and the entire curve group is one large prime-order group.

Examples:

* NIST P-256:

$$
h=1
$$

* secp256k1:

$$
h=1
$$

---

### 3. $n$ should not just be large; it should be close to $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$

Since:

$$
n=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{h},
$$

a small $h$ automatically makes $n$ close to the full group size.

For example:

If:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=8n,
$$

then the useful subgroup is only one-eighth of the curve group.

If:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=n,
$$

then every point is useful.

---

### 4. $p$ also matters

The field size $p$ determines the coordinate size and influences performance.

Typically you choose:

$$
p\approx 2^{256}
$$

for ~128-bit security.

Then Hasse gives:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)\approx p,
$$

so the group order is also about 256 bits.

---

### Putting it all together

The ideal cryptographic elliptic curve looks like:

$$
\boxed{
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=n
}
$$

where:

* $p$ is a large prime (field size),
* $n$ is a large prime (subgroup size),
* $h=1$,
* $G$ generates the entire curve group.

This is the "perfect" case.

When $h\neq1$, it is still fine if:

$$
h\text{ is small}
$$

and

$$
n\text{ is a large prime}.
$$

So the design priority is roughly:

1. **Large prime $n$** ✅ (security)
2. **Small cofactor $h$** ✅ (avoid subgroup issues)
3. Efficient choice of $p$, $a$, $b$, and $G$ ✅ (performance and implementation safety)

A useful mental shortcut is:

$$
\boxed{\text{ECC security comes from } n,\quad \text{while } h \text{ is something you want to keep harmless.}}
$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Since $n$ cannot be greater than $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$, therefore $h$ cannot be less then $1$. So $h$ must be **close** to $1$ really means $h$ should not be much greater than $1$ ie $n$ must be as large a percentage of $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ as possible.
>
> Also, since $p+1 - ∂ ≤ \\#E(\mathbb F_p) ≤ p+1 + ∂$, (where $∂ = 2 \cdot √p$), and  $n$ cannot be greater than $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$, therefore $n$, at the very most, can only be slightly larger than $p$, the size of the Field.

The ideal case:

$$
n=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

gives:

$$
h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}=1.
$$

If $h=2$:

$$
n=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{2},
$$

still very large.

If $h=8$:

$$
n=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{8},
$$

still fine for curves such as Ed25519.

So the hierarchy is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{First choose }p\text{ (field size)}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)\approx p\text{ by Hasse}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Choose }G\text{ so that }n\text{ is a large prime divisor of }\\#E(\mathbb F_p)
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Prefer small }h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}
}
$$

Your intuition was right: $n$ is constrained to be near $p$. The correction is just that "large $n$" means "as close as possible to $p$," not "much larger than $p$."
