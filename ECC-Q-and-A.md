> ## Q: What are G, n and h in the specification of an EC. eg See https://std.neuromancer.sk/nist/P-256

For an elliptic curve (EC) used in cryptography, the parameters **G**, **n**, and **h** define the subgroup in which cryptographic operations are performed. Using **NIST P-256** as an example:

* **G** — **Base point** (also called the **generator point**)

  * This is a specific point on the elliptic curve that everyone agrees to use.
  * Public keys are generated by multiplying this point by a private key:

    $$Q = dG$$

    where:
    * $d$ is the private key (an integer),
    * $Q$ is the public key (a point on the curve).

  On the P-256 specification page, **G** is given by its coordinates:

$$
G = (G_x, G_y)
$$
  
  where `Gx` and `Gy` are large hexadecimal numbers.

---

* **n** — **Order of the base point**

  * This is the number of distinct multiples of (G) before you return to the point at infinity.
  * Formally,

  $$nG = \mathcal{O}$$

    where $\mathcal{O}$ is the point at infinity (the identity element).

  For P-256,

$$
n = \texttt{FFFFFFFF00000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFBCE6FAADA7179E84F3B9CAC2FC632551}
$$

  which is a 256-bit prime.

  This means there are exactly $n$ points in the subgroup generated by $G$.

  The private key is chosen as

  $$
  1 \le d < n.
  $$

---

* **h** — **Cofactor**

  * The cofactor relates the size of the whole curve to the subgroup generated by $G$:

  $h = \frac{\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})}{n}$

    where $\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})$ is the total number of points on the curve (including the point at infinity).

  For P-256,

  $$h = 1$$

  This means:

  * every point on the curve is already in the subgroup generated by $G$,
  * there are no extra "small" subgroups to worry about,
  * implementations are simpler and safer.

---

### Why these parameters matter

Suppose Alice chooses a private key

$$
d = 123456789.
$$

Her public key is computed as

$$
Q = dG.
$$

Here:

* **G** tells everyone which generator to use.
* **n** tells Alice the valid range for $d$ and determines the subgroup size (which underpins the security level).
* **h** tells implementations whether they need to perform extra subgroup checks. Since P-256 has $h=1$, subgroup membership is straightforward.

---

### Summary

| Parameter | Meaning                                                            | P-256                   |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------| ----------------------- |
| **G**     | Generator (base) point used to derive public keys                  | Fixed point $(G_x,G_y)$ |
| **n**     | Order of $G$: smallest positive integer such that $nG=\mathcal{O}$ | Large 256-bit prime     |
| **h**     | Cofactor $=\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})/n$                                   | 1                       |

These three parameters, together with the field prime $p$ and the curve coefficients $a$ and $b$, completely specify the elliptic curve domain parameters used by protocols such as ECDSA and ECDH.


> ## Q: Will n always be prime for an EC?

No. **In general, the order $n$ of a point on an elliptic curve does not have to be prime.** However, in **cryptographic elliptic curves**, the chosen base point $G$ almost always has **prime order** (or an order that is a large prime times a very small cofactor).

It's helpful to distinguish three related quantities:

1. **The order of the curve**

$$
\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p),
$$
   
   which is the total number of points on the curve.

2. **The order of a point $P$**

$$
ord(P),
$$
   
   which is the smallest positive integer $m$ such that

$$
mP = \mathcal{O}.
$$

3. **The order $n$ in the domain parameters**
   This is specifically the order of the chosen generator $G$:

$$
n = ord(G).
$$

### A non-prime example

Suppose a curve has $\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = 60$

Then it might contain points of orders

* 2
* 3
* 4
* 5
* 6
* 10
* 12
* 15
* 20
* 30
* 60

depending on the group structure. If you chose a point of order 20 as your generator, then

$$
n = 20,
$$

which is **not prime**.

Mathematically, that's perfectly valid.

### Why cryptography prefers prime $n$

Most ECC schemes (ECDSA, ECDH, EdDSA, etc.) want the subgroup generated by $G$ to be **cyclic of large prime order** because:

* Every non-zero scalar has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$, which many algorithms rely on.
* There are no non-trivial subgroups inside a prime-order group, avoiding many small-subgroup attacks.
* The discrete logarithm problem has the expected hardness.

This is why standards typically choose

$$
\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = h \cdot n,
$$

where:

* $n$ is a **large prime**, and
* $h$ (the cofactor) is very small, usually 1, 2, 4, or 8.

For example:

| Curve      | $n$ prime? | Cofactor $h$ |
| ---------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| NIST P-256 | Yes        | 1            |
| secp256k1  | Yes        | 1            |
| Curve25519 | Yes        | 8            |
| Ed25519    | Yes        | 8            |

For Ed25519, the **entire curve group** is not prime-order—it has order $8n$—but the standard base point generates a subgroup of large prime order $n$.

So the answer is:

* **Mathematically:** No, $n$ can be composite.
* **In cryptographic standards:** $n$ is almost always chosen to be a large prime (or, equivalently, the generator is chosen to have large prime order), because that provides the algebraic properties and security required by ECC protocols.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Hasse-Weil bound aka Hasse's Theorem:
> 
> $p + 1 − 2p^{1/2} \leq \\#E\left(F_p \\!\\!​\\!\right) \leq p + 1 + 2p^{1/2}$
>
> Essentially, $\\#E(F_p) \approx p$ ie the number of elements in the Group $E(F_p)$ (points on the EC $E$ over the Field $F_p$) is roughly $p$, a little less or a little more.
>
> Also, $\\#E(F_p)$ is just another notation for $|E(F_p)|$
>
> Hence, Cardinality of $E(F_p)$ $\equiv$ $|E(F_p)| \equiv \\#E(F_p) \approx p = |F_p|$ $\equiv$ Cardinality of $F_p$
>
> $F_p$ is a Field (and hence a Group, because every Field is a Group). The operations are modular $\pmod{p}$ addition and modular $\pmod{p}$ multiplication.
>
> $E(F_p)$ is a Group (but not a Field). It contains the points on the EC $E$ over the Field $F_p$. The operation is "addition" of two points/elements.
>
> Field $F_p \equiv \left\\{0,1,2,...,p-1\right\\}$
>
> Group $E(F_p) \equiv \left\\{ (x,y) \in F_p \times F_p : (x,y) \text{ lies on } E \right\\}$
>


> ## Q: When you say "standards choose n to be a very large prime", it still can't be greater than p, correct? Because everything is (mod p).

Great question. The answer is **no: $n$ is not restricted to be less than $p$**. This is a very common point of confusion because the field arithmetic is done "mod $p$", but $n$ is a **group order**, not a field element.

Let's separate the two.

### 1. $p$ defines the field

The prime $p$ defines the field:

$$
\mathbb F_p = {0,1,\ldots,p-1}.
$$

All **coordinates** of elliptic curve points are elements of this field:

$$
x,y \in \mathbb F_p.
$$

So yes, coordinates are always reduced modulo $p$.

---

### 2. $n$ is the order of a group element

The value $n$ is defined by:

$$
nG = \mathcal O
$$

where $G$ is the generator point and $\mathcal O$ is the point at infinity.

This is repeated **point addition**:

$$
G,\quad 2G,\quad 3G,\quad \ldots,\quad nG=\mathcal O.
$$

The numbers $1,2,3,\ldots,n$ here are **integers counting how many times you add $G$**. They are not field elements.

So there is no rule saying:

$$
n < p.
$$

---

### Example

Take a small hypothetical curve:

$$
p=17.
$$

The field has:

$$
|\mathbb F_{17}|=17.
$$

The curve might have:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_{17})=19.
$$

A generator $G$ could have order:

$$
n=19.
$$

Here:

$$
n > p.
$$

Nothing is wrong. The curve group has 19 elements even though the field has only 17 elements.

The coordinates are modulo 17, but the number of points in the group can be larger.

---

### Why this happens

The field gives possible coordinates:

$$
(x,y)\in \mathbb F_p^2.
$$

There are $p^2$ possible pairs.

The curve selects some of those pairs, and Hasse's (bound) Theorem tells us:

$$
p+1 - ∂ ≤ \\#E(\mathbb F_p) ≤ p+1 + ∂
$$

where $∂ = 2√p$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> So the group order is naturally around $p$, but it can be slightly larger or smaller.
>

---

### Real example: NIST P-256

For P-256:

$$
p =
\texttt{FFFFFFFF00000001000000000000000000000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFF}
$$

and

$$
n =
\texttt{FFFFFFFF00000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFBCE6FAADA7179E84F3B9CAC2FC632551}
$$

Both are 256-bit numbers, but they are different. In fact, for P-256:

$$
n < p.
$$

For other curves, $n$ can be slightly larger than $p$. The only strict relationship is:

$$
n \mid \\#E(\mathbb F_p),
$$

because $n$ is the size of a subgroup of the elliptic curve group.

So the clean mental model is:

* $p$: size of the **field** (where coordinates live).
* $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$: size of the **elliptic curve group** (number of points).
* $n$: size of the **subgroup generated by $G$**.
* The phrase "mod $p$" applies to field arithmetic, not to the integer $n$.

> ## Q: So what are the ideal properties that n must have? Is the goal to make h as close to 1 as we can, or n as large as we can? Or something else?

Excellent question. The answer is: **both matter, but the primary goal is to make $n$ a very large prime and make the cofactor $h$ very small (ideally 1).** It is not simply "maximize $n$" or "make $h$ close to 1"; it is about achieving a secure group structure.

The ideal relationship is:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=h\cdot n
$$

where:

* $n$ = order of the subgroup generated by $G$
* $h$ = cofactor
* $G$ = generator of the subgroup

The desired properties are:

---

### 1. $n$ should be a large prime

This is the most important property.

Why?

Because ECC security relies on the **elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP)**:

Given:

$$
Q=dG
$$

it should be infeasible to find $d$.

The difficulty depends primarily on the size of the subgroup:

$$
|\langle G\rangle|=n.
$$

If $n$ is prime and very large:

* every non-zero private key $d$ has an inverse modulo $n$,
* there are no smaller subgroups inside $\langle G\rangle$,
* attacks are much harder.

For a 256-bit curve, you want roughly:

$$
n \approx 2^{256}
$$

with $n$ prime.

---

### 2. $h$ should be small (ideally $1$)

The cofactor is:

$$
h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}.
$$

A large $h$ means the curve has many points outside the subgroup generated by $G$.

Those extra subgroups can create problems, especially **small subgroup attacks**.

Ideal case:

$$
h=1
$$

Then:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=n
$$

and the entire curve group is one large prime-order group.

Examples:

* NIST P-256:

$$
h=1
$$

* secp256k1:

$$
h=1
$$

---

### 3. $n$ should not just be large; it should be close to $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$

Since:

$$
n=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{h},
$$

a small $h$ automatically makes $n$ close to the full group size.

For example:

If:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=8n,
$$

then the useful subgroup is only one-eighth of the curve group.

If:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=n,
$$

then every point is useful.

---

### 4. $p$ also matters

The field size $p$ determines the coordinate size and influences performance.

Typically you choose:

$$
p\approx 2^{256}
$$

for ~128-bit security.

Then Hasse gives:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)\approx p,
$$

so the group order is also about 256 bits.

---

### Putting it all together

The ideal cryptographic elliptic curve looks like:

$$
\boxed{
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=n
}
$$

where:

* $p$ is a large prime (field size),
* $n$ is a large prime (subgroup size),
* $h=1$,
* $G$ generates the entire curve group.

This is the "perfect" case.

When $h\neq1$, it is still fine if:

$$
h\text{ is small}
$$

and

$$
n\text{ is a large prime}.
$$

So the design priority is roughly:

1. **Large prime $n$** ✅ (security)
2. **Small cofactor $h$** ✅ (avoid subgroup issues)
3. Efficient choice of $p$, $a$, $b$, and $G$ ✅ (performance and implementation safety)

A useful mental shortcut is:

$$
\boxed{\text{ECC security comes from } n,\quad \text{while } h \text{ is something you want to keep harmless.}}
$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Since $n$ cannot be greater than $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$, therefore $h$ cannot be less then $1$. So $h$ must be **close** to $1$ really means $h$ should not be much greater than $1$ ie $n$ must be as large a percentage of $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ as possible.
>
> Also, since $p+1 - ∂ ≤ \\#E(\mathbb F_p) ≤ p+1 + ∂$, (where $∂ = 2 \cdot √p$), and  $n$ cannot be greater than $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$, therefore $n$, at the very most, can only be slightly larger than $p$, the size of the Field.

The ideal case:

$$
n=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

gives:

$$
h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}=1.
$$

If $h=2$:

$$
n=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{2},
$$

still very large.

If $h=8$:

$$
n=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{8},
$$

still fine for curves such as Ed25519.

So the hierarchy is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{First choose }p\text{ (field size)}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)\approx p\text{ by Hasse}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Choose }G\text{ so that }n\text{ is a large prime divisor of }\\#E(\mathbb F_p)
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Prefer small }h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}
}
$$

Your intuition was right: $n$ is constrained to be near $p$. The correction is just that "large $n$" means "as close as possible to $p$," not "much larger than $p$."

> ## Q: Oh okay I got it. So the goal to make n as large as I can and to make h as close to 1 are actually consistent with each other. I only have to guard against choosing a small n (compared to p), because that will result in a h quite larger than 1 (eg 2 or 3 etc).

Exactly. You've got the relationship now.

The two goals:

1. **Make $n$ as large as possible**
2. **Make $h$ as close to 1 as possible**

are actually the same goal viewed from two directions, because:

$$
h=\frac{\\#E(\mathbb F_p)}{n}
$$

and $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ is fixed once the curve is fixed.

For a given curve:

* If $n$ is large, the denominator is large, so $h$ is small.
* If $n$ is small, the denominator is small, so $h$ is large.

For example, suppose:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=1000.
$$

Possible subgroup choices:

| $n$  | $h=\frac{1000}{n}$ |
| ---- | ------------------ |
| 1000 | 1                  |
| 500  | 2                  |
| 250  | 4                  |
| 125  | 8                  |
| 10   | 100                |

The last case has a tiny subgroup and a huge cofactor, which is undesirable.

---

The important cryptographic requirement is not merely "large $n$" in an absolute sense, but:

$$
\boxed{n \text{ should be a very large prime}}
$$

because a composite $n$ can have small factors.

For example, imagine:

$$
n=2^{200}\cdot 3
$$

It is numerically enormous, but the subgroup contains a small subgroup of order 2 and 3. That can create security issues.

The ideal situation is:

$$
\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=h\cdot n
$$

where:

* $n$ is a large prime (the security parameter),
* $h$ is a very small integer (ideally 1).

So the design process is roughly:

1. Choose a field size $p$ (e.g., 256 bits).
2. Find a curve whose point count satisfies: $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=h \cdot n$
3. Ensure $n$ is a large prime.
4. Prefer $h=1$ or another tiny value. $h$ of course cannot be less than $1$, but if we can't have $h=1$, we want it to be greater than $1$ by as tiny an amount as possible.

Your summary is right:

> You don't want a small $n$ relative to $p$, because that means the usable cryptographic subgroup is only a small fraction of the curve group.

That is why cryptographic curves are not just random elliptic curves over $\mathbb F_p$; they are carefully selected so that the group structure has the right $n$ and $h$.

> ## Q: So is the subgroup of G only those points of E(Fp) that we can reach starting from G by group operations on G? And is it the same set that can be reached starting from group operations on two points G, H (H being any other point in the subgroup of G)?

Yes, your understanding is correct. Let's formalize it.

Given a point $G$ on an elliptic curve, the **subgroup generated by $G$** is:

$$
\langle G\rangle = { \mathcal O, G, 2G, 3G, 4G,\ldots,(n-1)G}
$$

where:

* $kG$ means adding $G$ to itself $k$ times.
* $n$ is the smallest positive integer such that: $nG=\mathcal O$
* Therefore, $\langle G\rangle$ contains exactly $n$ points.

So yes:

> The subgroup of $G$ is exactly the set of points reachable by repeatedly applying the group operation to $G$.

---

### What if we start from another point $H$ in the subgroup?

Suppose:

$$
H \in \langle G\rangle.
$$

That means:

$$
H=aG
$$

for some integer $a$.

Now consider the subgroup generated by $G$ and $H$:

$$
\langle G,H\rangle.
$$

This consists of all combinations:

$$
iG+jH
$$

for integers $i,j$.

Substitute $H=aG$:

$$
iG+j(aG)
$$

Using the group law:

$$
=(i+ja)G.
$$

But $(i+ja)G$ is still just another multiple of $G$, so:

$$
\langle G,H\rangle \subseteq \langle G\rangle.
$$

The reverse inclusion is obvious because $G$ is already included. Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\langle G,H\rangle=\langle G\rangle}
$$

So yes: **starting with $G$ and any other point $H$ that is already in the subgroup does not give you any new points.**

---

### A concrete analogy

Think of the integers modulo 12 under addition:

$$
\mathbb Z_{12}
$$

Take:

$$
G=1
$$

Repeated addition gives:

$$
\langle1\rangle={0,1,2,3,\ldots,11}
$$

Now choose:

$$
H=5
$$

Since $5\in\langle1\rangle$,

$$
\langle1,5\rangle
$$

is still the whole group. Adding 5 as an extra "generator" does not expand anything.

But if you chose a point outside the subgroup (in a larger group), then adding that point as a generator could create a larger subgroup.

---

### ECC context

For a cryptographic curve:

$$
E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

may contain many points, but $G$ generates only a subgroup:

$$
\langle G\rangle
$$

The size of this subgroup is:

$$
|\langle G\rangle|=n
$$

The cofactor describes how much larger the full group is:

$$
|E(\mathbb F_p)|=h \cdot n
$$

When $h=1$:

$$
\langle G\rangle=E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

meaning every point on the curve can be reached from $G$.

When $h>1$, there are points on the curve that are **not reachable** from $G$, and they belong to other cosets/subgroups. The cryptographic operations stay inside $\langle G\rangle$, because that is the large prime-order subgroup chosen for security.

> ## Q: So given E(Fp), we choose a G that gives us the biggest size subgroup, correct?

Yes, that is essentially correct — with one important refinement.

Given an elliptic curve group $E(\mathbb F_p)$, we want to choose a point (G) whose generated subgroup $\langle G\rangle$ is as large as possible **and has a desirable order** (ideally a large prime order).

The ideal situation is:

$$
\langle G\rangle = E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

so that:

$$
|\langle G\rangle| = |E(\mathbb F_p)|
$$

and therefore:

$$
h=\frac{|E(\mathbb F_p)|}{n}=1.
$$

In this case, every point on the curve is reachable from $G$.

---

However, there is a subtle point: **you do not choose $G$ arbitrarily to maximize size.** The curve's group structure determines what subgroup sizes are possible.

For any point $P$: $\mathrm{ord}(P) \mid |E(\mathbb F_p)|$ (by Lagrange's theorem).

So if $|E(\mathbb F_p)|=1000=2^3\cdot5^3$, then no point can have order, say, 999. The possible orders are constrained by the factors of the group order.

---

The cryptographic design process is roughly:

1. Choose $p$ and the curve parameters $a,b$.
2. Compute: $|E(\mathbb F_p)|$.

3. Factor the group order: $|E(\mathbb F_p)|=h\cdot n$.

4. Find a point $G$ with: $\mathrm{ord}(G)=n$ where $n$ is a large prime.

So $G$ is not just "the point that gives the largest subgroup"; it is specifically chosen to generate the **large prime-order subgroup**.

---

For example, suppose:

$$
|E(\mathbb F_p)|=8n
$$

where $n$ is a 256-bit prime.

Then the curve has:

* a large subgroup of size $n$,
* some smaller leftover structure of size related to the cofactor $8$.

We choose:

$$
G\in E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

such that:

$$
\langle G\rangle
$$

has size:

$$
n
$$

---

So the refined statement is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Choose }G\text{ to generate the largest useful subgroup, i.e. the largest prime-order subgroup.}
}
$$

Not necessarily the absolutely largest subgroup if the full group order is composite — what matters is that the subgroup order (n) is **large and prime**. When the full group order is prime ($h=1$), these two goals coincide.

===

> ## Q: So ord(P) is the size of the subgroup generated by P, correct?

Yes, exactly.

The **order of a point $P$**, written:

$$
\mathrm{ord}(P),
$$

is the number of elements in the subgroup generated by $P$:

$$
\langle P\rangle
$$

More formally:

$$
\mathrm{ord}(P)=\min{m>0 : mP=\mathcal O}
$$

where:

* $mP$ means adding $P$ to itself $m$ times,
* $\mathcal O$ is the point at infinity (the identity element).

The subgroup generated by $P$ is:

$$
\langle P\rangle =
{\mathcal O, P, 2P, 3P,\ldots,(m-1)P}.
$$

There are exactly $m$ elements in this set, so:

$$
\boxed{|\langle P\rangle|=\mathrm{ord}(P)}
$$

---

For example, suppose:

$$
\mathrm{ord}(P)=5.
$$

Then:

$$
\langle P\rangle =
{\mathcal O, P, 2P, 3P, 4P}
$$

and:

$$
5P=\mathcal O.
$$

After that, the sequence repeats:

$$
6P=P,\quad 7P=2P,\quad\ldots
$$

---

This is why in ECC the generator (G) is specified together with its order $n$:

$$
\mathrm{ord}(G)=n.
$$

The private key $d$ is an integer:

$$
1\le d<n,
$$

and the public key is:

$$
Q=dG.
$$

Because $G$ has order $n$, all possible public keys stay inside the subgroup:

$$
\langle G\rangle
$$

which contains exactly $n$ points.

> ## Q: 1. Will the largest prime divisor of #E(Fp) always be the n that gives a G with the largest subgroup? <br><br> 2. Regardless of the answer to (1), Isn't it easier (in terms of Time & Space Complexity) to first choose the largest prime divisor of #E(Fp) and then find G, and then move to the next largest prime divisor of #E(Fp), until we find the largest subgroup? Or is it same Time & Space Complexity to first choose G and get n? Or is it the case that for some curves G -> n is easier and for others n -> G is easier in terms of Time & Space Complexity? <br><br> 3. Is it possible that n is a prime divisor of #E(Fp), m is a composite divisor of #E(Fp) and the subgroup enforced by m is larger than the subgroup enforced by n? If so, then which is better to select: n or m? <br><br> 4. Is it possible that for two numbers n1 and n2 (regardless prime or not), the subgroup enforced by the smaller number is larger?

These are excellent questions because they get into the distinction between **subgroup order as a number** and **which points actually generate those subgroups**. Let's go one by one.

---

## 1. Will the largest prime divisor of $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ always be the $n$ that gives a $G$ with the largest subgroup?

**Almost, but with an important qualification.**

Let: $N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$

Suppose: $N=ab$ where $a$ is the largest prime divisor of $N$.

A subgroup generated by a point $G$ can have order equal to **any divisor of $N$** that is possible in the group structure. Therefore, the largest possible subgroup order is actually $N$ itself (if the group is cyclic and a generator exists), not necessarily the largest prime divisor.

Example:

Let $N=15=3\cdot5$

The largest prime divisor is $5$, but a point of order $15$ would generate a larger subgroup $|\langle G\rangle|=15$

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{largest prime divisor} \neq \text{largest possible subgroup order}}
$$

---

However, ECC usually wants:

$$
n=\text{large prime}
$$

not merely the largest subgroup.

That is why standards often choose:

$$
N=h \cdot n
$$

where $n$ is the large prime factor and $h$ (the cofactor discussed earlier) is small.

---

## 2. Is it easier to choose $n$ first and find $G$, or choose $G$ and find $n$?

In practice:

$$
\boxed{n\rightarrow G\text{ is easier}}
$$

The reason is that the order of a random point is not immediately obvious.

Suppose you know:

$$
N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)
$$

You factor it:

$$
N=h \cdot n
$$

You choose the desired subgroup order $n$.

Then you can find $G$ by:

1. Pick a random point $P$.
2. Compute: $G=hP$.

Why does this work?

Because multiplying by $h$ removes the unwanted cofactor component. The resulting point often lands in the subgroup of order $n$.

Then verify:

$$
nG=\mathcal O
$$

---

The reverse approach:

> Pick $G$, then determine $\mathrm{ord}(G)$

is harder because computing the order of a random point requires knowing the factorization of $N$ anyway.

You would typically do:

$$
N,\text{ factor }N,\text{ test divisors}.
$$

So it is not really a different problem.

---

## 3. Can a prime divisor $n$ give a smaller subgroup than a composite divisor $m$?

Yes, absolutely.

Example:

Let $N=1000$

Possible subgroup orders: $n=5$ and $m=200$.

The subgroup generated by a point of order $200$ has: $200$ points.

The subgroup generated by a point of order $5$ has: $5$ points.

So: $m>n$ gives a larger subgroup.

However, ECC usually prefers:

$$
n= \text{large prime}
$$

over:

$$
m=\text{large composite}.
$$

Why?

Because a composite-order group has internal structure: $m=m_1 \cdot m_2\cdots$ and attackers can exploit small factors.

For example: $m=2^{200}\cdot3$

Although huge, it contains tiny subgroups of order 2 and 3.

So the usual choice is:

$$
\boxed{\text{large prime order }n \text{ over larger composite order}}
$$

---

## 4. Can a smaller number have a larger subgroup than a larger number?

No.

The order of a point **is exactly the size of its subgroup**:

$$
|\langle P\rangle|=\mathrm{ord}(P)
$$

So if:

$$
n_1<n_2
$$

and both are actual subgroup orders, then:

$$
|\langle P_1\rangle| < |\langle P_2\rangle|
$$

There is no situation where the smaller subgroup order produces a larger subgroup.

---

### The key hierarchy

It helps to separate three concepts:

1. **Curve group size** $N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$

2. **Subgroup size generated by a point** $|\langle G\rangle|=\mathrm{ord}(G)$

3. **Cryptographic subgroup order** $n$

where $n$ is chosen to be:

* very large,
* prime,
* close to $N$.

The ideal case:

$$
N=n
$$

so:

$$
h=1
$$

More commonly:

$$
N=h \cdot n
$$

with small $h$.

So the practical workflow is not:

$$
G\rightarrow n
$$

but rather:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Choose curve } \rightarrow N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)
\rightarrow \text{factor }N
\rightarrow \text{choose large prime }n
\rightarrow \text{find }G
}
$$

which is exactly how cryptographic curves are designed.

> ## Q: So for any n we pick (without restriction, prime or not), the size of the resulting subgroup will be equal to n?

Almost, but there is one crucial restriction.

You **cannot pick an arbitrary integer $n$** and expect there to be a point of that order.

The correct statement is:

> If there exists a point $G$ with
> $\mathrm{ord}(G)=n$,
> then the subgroup generated by $G$ has exactly $n$ elements.

That is,

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{ord}(G)=|\langle G\rangle|}
$$

So the subgroup size is always equal to the order of its generator.

---

### But not every $n$ is possible

Suppose $|E(\mathbb F_p)|=60$

By Lagrange's theorem, every subgroup order must divide 60.

Possible subgroup orders include: $1,2,3,4,5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60$.

Impossible subgroup orders include:

$$
7,11,13,17,25,61,\ldots
$$

because none of those divide 60.

So you cannot say:

> "I'd like a subgroup of order 17."

There simply cannot be one.

---

### One more subtlety

Even if $n$ divides $|E(\mathbb F_p)|$, **there may or may not be a subgroup of order $n$** depending on the group structure.

For elliptic curve groups over finite fields, however, a very useful theorem comes to the rescue.

If

$$
|E(\mathbb F_p)|=N,
$$

then

$$
E(\mathbb F_p)\cong \mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_n
$$

for suitable integers $m$ and $n$ with $m\mid n$.

From this structure, one can determine exactly which point orders occur.

In the cryptographic curves used in practice, the desired large prime divisor **does** correspond to a subgroup, so a generator 4G$ of that subgroup exists.

---

So the clean statement is:

* If $G$ has order $n$, then $|\langle G\rangle|=n$.
* But you cannot choose $n$ arbitrarily; $n$ must be a realizable point order for that particular elliptic curve group.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In cryptographic standards, the designers first ensure that the curve has a large prime-order subgroup, and then they publish a generator $G$ of exactly that subgroup.
>

> ## Q: Let me rephrase by previous question: 1. Will the largest prime divisor of #E(Fp) always result in a subgroup? Or we may need to try the 2nd largest prime divisor and so on till we get a subgroup? <br><br> 2. And I assume, out of all prime divisors that result in a subgroup, the largest one will result in the largest subgroup, correct? (Going by the fact that ord(G)=n implies |<G>| = n)

These are very good questions. The answer to (1) is actually a nice theorem from finite abelian group theory.

### 1. Will the largest prime divisor of $\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ always result in a subgroup?

**Yes.**

Let $N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$, and let (q) be a prime divisor of $N$.

A theorem called **Cauchy's theorem for finite groups** says:

> If a prime $q$ divides the order of a finite group, then the group contains an element of order $q$.

Since $E(\mathbb F_p)$ is a finite group, if $q \mid N$, then there is **guaranteed** to exist a point $G$ such that $\mathrm{ord}(G)=q$.

Therefore:

* the **largest prime divisor** of $N$ always occurs as the order of some point,
* so you do **not** have to try the second-largest prime divisor.

The caveat is that a subgroup of order **composite** $m$ is not guaranteed merely because $m\mid N$ (though for elliptic curve groups, the structure is well understood and one can determine which composite orders occur). But for **prime** divisors, Cauchy's theorem gives you the guarantee.

---

### 2. Of all prime divisors that result in subgroups, does the largest one give the largest prime-order subgroup?

Yes.

Suppose the prime divisors are $3,5,17,101$.

Then Cauchy's theorem guarantees subgroups of orders $3,5,17,101$.

Since $|\langle G\rangle|=\mathrm{ord}(G)$, the subgroup of order 101 is larger than the subgroup of order 17, and so on.

So among **prime-order subgroups**, the one corresponding to the largest prime divisor is indeed the largest.

---

### One important distinction

Be careful not to confuse:

* **largest prime-order subgroup**, and
* **largest subgroup overall**.

Example:

$$
|E(\mathbb F_p)|=210=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7.
$$

Then Cauchy's theorem guarantees subgroups of orders

$$
2,3,5,7.
$$

The largest **prime-order** subgroup has order 7.

But there may also exist a subgroup of order

$$
35,70,105,\text{or even }210,
$$

depending on the group's structure.

Those are **larger** subgroups, but they are **not prime-order**.

ECC standards usually choose a large **prime-order** subgroup because prime-order groups have especially desirable algebraic and security properties.

So your statement is correct with the qualifier "among prime-order subgroups":

$$
\boxed{\text{The largest prime divisor of }|E(\mathbb F_p)|\text{ gives the largest prime-order subgroup.}}
$$

And thanks to Cauchy's theorem, such a subgroup is guaranteed to exist.

> ## Q: So (1) means we don't really have to iterate over the prime divisors in descending order. We can just take n as the largest prime divisor, correct? <br><br> Also, for (2), of course I meant prime-order subgroups. Because I am only comparing the sizes of subgroups resulting from prime divisors, and they will all have prime orders since ord(G)=n implies |<G>| = n.

Yes, both of those statements are correct.

### 1. No need to iterate over the prime divisors

Suppose $N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$, and its prime factorization is $N=2^3\cdot 5\cdot 17\cdot q$, where $q$ is the largest prime factor.

By **Cauchy's theorem**, since $q\mid N$, there **must** exist a point $G$ of order $q$.

So if your goal is:

> "Find the largest prime-order subgroup,"

then you can simply take

$$
\boxed{n=q}
$$

and then find a point $G$ with

$$
\mathrm{ord}(G)=q.
$$

There is no need to test $17$, then $5$, etc., because you already know the subgroup of order $q$ exists.

### 2. Yes, the largest prime divisor gives the largest prime-order subgroup

Exactly.

If the prime divisors are $3,5,17,101$, then there are subgroups of sizes $3,5,17,101$.

So your reasoning is perfectly sound.

---

### One practical note

The only remaining task is **finding a point $G$ of order $n$**. Knowing that such a point exists (by Cauchy's theorem) doesn't immediately tell you *which* point it is.

A standard method is:

1. Compute $N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)=hn$.
2. Pick a random point $P\in E(\mathbb F_p)$.
3. Compute $G=hP$.
4. If $G\neq\mathcal O$, then (when $n$ is prime) $G$ has order $n$.

If $G=\mathcal O$, pick another random point $P$ and try again.

> [!NOTE]
> This is why standards such as NIST P-256 publish a specific generator $G$: the work of finding a suitable generator has already been done during the curve's construction.
>

> ## Q: So seems the most difficult part is to find #E(Fp​). Once we do that, then a) factor #E(Fp​) and take the largest prime factor as n, or b) check if #E(Fp​) itself is prime and take n = #E(Fp​). <br><br> Then we "divide" #E(Fp​) by n to get h. Yes, that's essentially the workflow used in elliptic curve construction. There is just one important refinement to step (4).

Here's the complete picture.

1. **Choose the field** $\mathbb F_p$ and the curve $E$.

2. **Compute the group order** $N=\\#E(\mathbb F_p)$. This is indeed the mathematically hardest part. Efficient algorithms such as Schoof's algorithm and its improvements (SEA) were developed specifically for this problem.

3. **Factor $N$.**

4. **Choose $n$.**

   * If $N$ itself is prime, then $n=N,\qquad h=1$.
   * Otherwise, choose the **largest prime factor** of $N$: $n=\text{largest prime divisor of }N$.

5. **Compute the cofactor** $h=\frac{N}{n}$.

6. **Find a generator $G$.**

   Pick a random point $P\in E(\mathbb F_p)$ and compute $G=hP$.

   Now there are two possibilities:

   * If $G=\mathcal O$, then pick another random $P$.

   * If $G\neq\mathcal O$, then **because $n$ is prime**, $G$ automatically has order $n$.

   This last point is the refinement.

---

### Why does $G\neq\mathcal O$ imply $\mathrm{ord}(G)=n$?

Since $N=hn$, we have $n(hP)=NhP=\mathcal O$.

So the order of $G=hP$ divides $n$.

But $n$ is **prime**.

Therefore the only possible orders are:

* $1$, or
* $n$.

If $G=\mathcal O$, its order is $1$.

If $G\neq\mathcal O$, its order cannot be $1$, so it **must** be $n$.

That's why the simple test $G\neq\mathcal O$ is sufficient.

---

So your summary is almost exactly right. The only addition I'd make is this conclusion:

> Once $n$ is chosen to be prime and $h=N/n$, **any non-identity point of the form $hP$ is automatically a generator of the subgroup of order $n$.**

That's a very elegant consequence of the primality of $n$, and it's one of the reasons cryptographic curves are designed this way.
