> ## Q: What are G, n and h in the specification of an EC. eg See https://std.neuromancer.sk/nist/P-256

For an elliptic curve (EC) used in cryptography, the parameters **G**, **n**, and **h** define the subgroup in which cryptographic operations are performed. Using **NIST P-256** as an example:

* **G** — **Base point** (also called the **generator point**)

  * This is a specific point on the elliptic curve that everyone agrees to use.
  * Public keys are generated by multiplying this point by a private key:

    $$Q = dG$$

    where:
    * $d$ is the private key (an integer),
    * $Q$ is the public key (a point on the curve).

  On the P-256 specification page, **G** is given by its coordinates:

$$
G = (G_x, G_y)
$$
  
  where `Gx` and `Gy` are large hexadecimal numbers.

---

* **n** — **Order of the base point**

  * This is the number of distinct multiples of (G) before you return to the point at infinity.
  * Formally,

  $$nG = \mathcal{O}$$

    where $\mathcal{O}$ is the point at infinity (the identity element).

  For P-256,

$$
n = \texttt{FFFFFFFF00000000FFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFBCE6FAADA7179E84F3B9CAC2FC632551}
$$

  which is a 256-bit prime.

  This means there are exactly $n$ points in the subgroup generated by $G$.

  The private key is chosen as

  $$
  1 \le d < n.
  $$

---

* **h** — **Cofactor**

  * The cofactor relates the size of the whole curve to the subgroup generated by $G$:

  $h = \frac{\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})}{n}$

    where $\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})$ is the total number of points on the curve (including the point at infinity).

  For P-256,

  $$h = 1$$

  This means:

  * every point on the curve is already in the subgroup generated by $G$,
  * there are no extra "small" subgroups to worry about,
  * implementations are simpler and safer.

---

### Why these parameters matter

Suppose Alice chooses a private key

$$
d = 123456789.
$$

Her public key is computed as

$$
Q = dG.
$$

Here:

* **G** tells everyone which generator to use.
* **n** tells Alice the valid range for $d$ and determines the subgroup size (which underpins the security level).
* **h** tells implementations whether they need to perform extra subgroup checks. Since P-256 has $h=1$, subgroup membership is straightforward.

---

### Summary

| Parameter | Meaning                                                            | P-256                   |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------| ----------------------- |
| **G**     | Generator (base) point used to derive public keys                  | Fixed point $(G_x,G_y)$ |
| **n**     | Order of $G$: smallest positive integer such that $nG=\mathcal{O}$ | Large 256-bit prime     |
| **h**     | Cofactor $=\\#E(\mathbb{F_p})/n$                                   | 1                       |

These three parameters, together with the field prime $p$ and the curve coefficients $a$ and $b$, completely specify the elliptic curve domain parameters used by protocols such as ECDSA and ECDH.


> ## Q: Will n always be prime for an EC?

No. **In general, the order $n$ of a point on an elliptic curve does not have to be prime.** However, in **cryptographic elliptic curves**, the chosen base point $G$ almost always has **prime order** (or an order that is a large prime times a very small cofactor).

It's helpful to distinguish three related quantities:

1. **The order of the curve**

$$
\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p),
$$
   
   which is the total number of points on the curve.

2. **The order of a point $P$**

$$
ord(P),
$$
   
   which is the smallest positive integer $m$ such that

$$
mP = \mathcal{O}.
$$

3. **The order $n$ in the domain parameters**
   This is specifically the order of the chosen generator $G$:

$$
n = ord(G).
$$

### A non-prime example

Suppose a curve has $\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = 60$

Then it might contain points of orders

* 2
* 3
* 4
* 5
* 6
* 10
* 12
* 15
* 20
* 30
* 60

depending on the group structure. If you chose a point of order 20 as your generator, then

$$
n = 20,
$$

which is **not prime**.

Mathematically, that's perfectly valid.

### Why cryptography prefers prime $n$

Most ECC schemes (ECDSA, ECDH, EdDSA, etc.) want the subgroup generated by $G$ to be **cyclic of large prime order** because:

* Every non-zero scalar has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$, which many algorithms rely on.
* There are no non-trivial subgroups inside a prime-order group, avoiding many small-subgroup attacks.
* The discrete logarithm problem has the expected hardness.

This is why standards typically choose

$$
\\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = h \cdot n,
$$

where:

* $n$ is a **large prime**, and
* $h$ (the cofactor) is very small, usually 1, 2, 4, or 8.

For example:

| Curve      | $n$ prime? | Cofactor $h$ |
| ---------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| NIST P-256 | Yes        | 1            |
| secp256k1  | Yes        | 1            |
| Curve25519 | Yes        | 8            |
| Ed25519    | Yes        | 8            |

For Ed25519, the **entire curve group** is not prime-order—it has order $8n$—but the standard base point generates a subgroup of large prime order $n$.

So the answer is:

* **Mathematically:** No, $n$ can be composite.
* **In cryptographic standards:** $n$ is almost always chosen to be a large prime (or, equivalently, the generator is chosen to have large prime order), because that provides the algebraic properties and security required by ECC protocols.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Hasse-Weil bound aka Hasse's Theorem:
> 
> $p + 1 − 2p^{1/2} \leq \\#E\left(F_p \\!\\!​\\!\right) \leq p + 1 + 2p^{1/2}$
>
> Essentially, $\\#E(F_p) \approx p$ ie the number of elements in the Group $E(F_p)$ (points on the EC $E$ over the Field $F_p$) is roughly $p$, a little less or a little more.
>
> Also, $\\#E(F_p)$ is just another notation for $|E(F_p)|$
>
> Hence, Cardinality of $E(F_p)$ $\equiv$ $|E(F_p)| \equiv \\#E(F_p) \approx p = |F_p|$ $\equiv$ Cardinality of $F_p$
>
> $F_p$ is a Field (and hence a Group, because every Field is a Group). The operations are modular $\pmod{p}$ addition and modular $\pmod{p}$ multiplication.
>
> $E(F_p)$ is a Group (but not a Field). It contains the points on the EC $E$ over the Field $F_p$. The operation is "addition" of two points/elements.
>
> Field $F_p \equiv \left\\{0,1,2,...,p-1\right\\}$
>
> Group $E(F_p) \equiv \left\\{ (x,y) \in F_p \times F_p : (x,y) \text{ lies on } E \right\\}$
>
